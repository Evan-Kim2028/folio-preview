Folio Club: A Protocol-Owned Gacha House for Tokenized Trading Cards

Abstract#

Here is the simplest version of what Folio Club does: you pay a fixed price for a ticket, say $100. Before you buy, the app shows you the full odds — every possible card you could win, its probability, and its value. If you miss the big prizes, you still get at least $13 back, guaranteed. No one can change those odds after you click buy, and no one — not even the people who run the protocol — can see the result before the random draw happens.

That is a trading-card gacha game: pay a fixed price, get a random tokenized (blockchain-recorded) trading card, and either keep it, redeem the physical graded card, or sell it back on the spot. This is already a large business on Solana. In every version shipped so far, a company runs the house: it sets the odds privately, holds the randomness, prices the prizes, and keeps the profit. Folio Club replaces the company with a pool that anyone can join.

Two ways in. First, as a liquidity provider (LP) — someone who deposits money into the shared bankroll that pays out prizes. Deposit USDC, get shares priced at net asset value (NAV, the pool's total value divided by the number of shares), and earn your slice of the house's edge over time. Second, as a consignor — someone who lists a physical trading card with the protocol instead of selling it outright. The card sits in escrow (held by a neutral custodian, not owned or sold) until it is either won by a player or returned to you. You get paid the moment your card is drawn, or you can take payment in shares and become an LP too.

Three design choices make this pool safe enough for outside money to trust it, where a company-run house is not. First, every ticket's full odds table is calculated on-chain (computed and stored directly by the blockchain program, not a private server) from live inventory and live prices, and locked in before the random draw exists — so no one can build the odds around a result they already know. Second, card prices come from an oracle (a price feed the blockchain program reads from) with clearly stated limits on how stale or uncertain a price can be before it's rejected, and prices only enter the game through a discount and a competitive waiting line, never because a seller just says what their card is worth. Third, the house cannot back out of a bet: settlement pays out strictly in the order tickets were bought, against a snapshot frozen at purchase time, and the worst thing that can happen is a delayed draw that refunds you.

The same three choices give players a number no competitor publishes: return to play (RTP) — the share of every ticket dollar that comes back to players as prizes, targeted at 92.5% here — and the full list of possible outcomes, readable on-chain before you buy. A miss still guarantees at least 13% of your money back. Profits from the house edge flow into the value of an LP share, not into a separate reward token that inflates over time.

1. Introduction#

Running the house — being the side of the bet that wins on average — is the profitable side of any gacha game. Every platform shipped so far reserves that seat for a company. Folio Club opens that seat to anyone.

The product itself is simple: physical graded trading cards, tokenized on Solana, turned into a gacha machine. Pay a fixed price, get a random card, then keep it, redeem the physical card, or sell it back. The money behind it is not small. The category sold over a billion dollars of packs in eighteen months; Collector Crypt alone booked $146.9M in Q1 2026, about 98% of it from gacha. Card owners today can pay marketplace fees, list cards for sale, or buy tickets — but they cannot put up bankroll and earn a share of the house's edge. The industry has tokenized the cards, the storage, and the settlement, but left the one position that reliably makes money closed to outsiders.

That seat has stayed closed because nobody outside the operator can verify the house's edge — and you can't put your own money behind a bet you can't check. RipIndex, an independent on-chain tracker, follows seven platforms and measures every draw against the platform's own cash-out rate. Collector Crypt comes out to −5.4% realized edge across 923,587 draws — meaning players get back 5.4% less than they put in, on average — with individual machines ranging from −21.7% to +10.6%. RipIndex had to reverse-engineer these numbers because Collector Crypt does not publish its odds anywhere. The pattern repeats: GachaPull advertises +2.0% but delivers −5.1%; Courtyard delivers −17.9%; Beezie can't be measured at all because it publishes no per-draw data. The number a platform advertises and the number a player actually receives are two different things everywhere, and the gap between them — largely the cash-out haircut — is exactly what nobody discloses.

To be fair to the trackers: every one of these figures is an estimate, not a certainty. RipIndex infers odds from what actually happened, averages across machines that may have been retuned mid-measurement, and marks each draw at a buyback rate the operator sets and can change. It's a careful reconstruction of information a platform could have simply published. And to be fair to the platforms: a billion dollars in sales says players will buy in even without that disclosure. But an outside investor cannot back a bet whose odds are a private number, and a player cannot tell a −21.7% machine from a +10.6% one until someone has tracked a million draws on it.

Folio Club's fix is to make the odds a property anyone can check on-chain, instead of a claim you have to trust the operator on. Every ticket's full odds table is calculated on-chain from live inventory, targeting a return-to-play (RTP) of 92.5% ± 0.5% — the same band on every table, so there's no secretly generous or secretly stingy machine — with a guaranteed 13% floor on a miss. That table is locked into the ticket's on-chain record before the random draw exists. Randomness comes from a verifiable on-chain source with a proof attached, settlement pays out strictly in purchase order against the frozen snapshot, and there's no way for the house to see an outcome and refuse it (§6). A player gets what no competitor offers: the exact RTP and full list of possible outcomes for the ticket in front of them, readable before purchase, with at least 13% guaranteed back on a miss. LPs get the thing that makes it safe to pool money into a shared bankroll at all (§4).

One part of the system still runs on trust: card prices come from an oracle, and at launch that oracle has a single, disclosed publisher. A bad price from that publisher could misvalue an item and skew the odds of tables built afterward (§5). But the failure mode is narrow: this house can misjudge what a prize is worth; it cannot choose who wins. Spreading the pricing role across multiple publishers is the planned next step to close that gap.

Three earlier projects shaped this design, each by getting one thing wrong. FWA (on Ethereum) proved a permissionless house — anyone can be the bank — works, but let depositors say what their own asset was worth with no outside check, and propped up returns with a token that paid out 1% a day in new supply. Collector Crypt proved the product works at scale — tokenized graded cards, real storage, gacha that people actually buy — but kept the house closed and the odds off-chain. OlympusDAO proved a protocol can own its own liquidity, and also proved that wrapping the idea in a token designed to be staked for more of itself eventually collapses it. Folio Club combines the parts that worked and drops the parts that didn't: Collector Crypt's product, FWA's open-to-anyone house, Olympus's protocol-owned bankroll — with prices from an oracle instead of self-reported values, yield from real profit instead of new token supply, and odds on-chain instead of on a private server.

Sources: all realized-edge figures and draw counts come from RipIndex, read at https://rip-index.com/ on 2026-08-06 (the site updates continuously, so treat these as a snapshot). RipIndex values each draw at the platform's own instant-buyback rate — the probability-weighted buyback value of the draw, divided by the pack price — not at market value, which is why its figures sit below the platforms' own advertised numbers. Collector Crypt's advertised return and Q1 2026 revenue are its own published figures; the randomness-commitment analysis is drawn from its open-source cc-vrf repository and live on-chain inspection. Full source list at the end of this document.

2. Architecture#

The protocol is three Anchor programs (Anchor is the standard framework for writing Solana smart contracts). Splitting them apart is a security decision: the money (vault) and the game logic (engine) sit behind a narrow interface, and the price feed is a plug-in that either program can read but neither can fake.

A "crank" is a permissionless bot or script that performs routine on-chain maintenance — building a draw table, settling a ticket, closing an expired listing — and earns a small tip for doing it. Anyone can run one.

At genesis, one disclosed party wears every operator hat: treasury custodian, price publisher, crank runner, and reference frontend (the default app UI). None of those roles is meant to stay exclusive. Cranks are open to anyone from day one and pay for themselves; the price publisher is a replaceable plug-in behind the oracle registry (§5); and the reference frontend competes with any other app built on the same programs, on equal terms. Treasury-held escrow — the operator physically holding consigned cards — is the one real trust cost at genesis, and it has an on-chain exit path (§7.2). These roles are combined for now because the protocol is new, not because the design requires it: separating them out is an operational milestone, not a rewrite.

Governance at genesis is a multisig (a wallet that requires multiple people to sign off on an action). Every dial in Appendix A is a configurable genesis parameter with a stated bound — governance can move it, but only inside a published range, and every proposed change is visible on-chain before it takes effect. The planned successor is futarchy via MetaDAO: a governance token, raised through a MetaDAO token sale once the protocol has real mainnet volume, that claims the protocol-fee treasury (§9.2) and steers the bounded dials through prediction markets instead of votes. The token never touches the yield loop itself: HOUSE shares keep the only claim on the vault and the edge, the token only owns the fee treasury, and any futarchy proposal is still limited to the same stated bounds. That is the Olympus lesson kept intact through a token launch: the governance market prices the fee line, not the house.

3. Notation#

Symbols are defined at first use. A full table is in Appendix C; reference parameter values (governance-settable unless noted) are in Appendix A.

4. The house vault#

The vault holds USDC, full stop. Its financial state is two numbers: how much cash it has (C), and how much of that cash is locked against open bets (L).

4.1 Shares and NAV#

When you deposit, you get shares priced at the pool's net asset value (NAV) — the pool's total value divided by the number of shares outstanding. A deposit of d USDC mints

\Delta S = \frac{d \cdot S}{V}

shares (the very first deposit mints 1:1), and withdrawing \Delta S shares redeems \Delta S \cdot \nu USDC at the NAV in effect at the moment you withdraw. The pool's value is read from numbers the program itself tracks, never from a live wallet balance — so no one can inflate NAV by donating tokens directly to the vault address, a known attack against pool designs that read balances directly (the "ERC-4626 inflation attack").

There is no separate reward token in the yield loop. A HOUSE share is a direct claim on the vault's assets. The house's profit shows up as a rising NAV per share — nothing sits between the edge and the shareholder.

4.2 Withdrawals#

Withdrawing is two steps: request, then wait out a cooldown (genesis value: 7 days), then execute at whatever NAV is current at that moment. The cooldown exists for two reasons: it stops an LP from timing a withdrawal to dodge a jackpot payout that's about to land, and money that's locked against open tickets (L) can never be withdrawn — a withdrawal can only draw against C - L, the cash that isn't already promised to a bet in progress. Your capital stays in play for the entire cooldown.

5. The oracle registry#

Every card resolves to a price feed: a price, a confidence width (how uncertain that price is), a last-update time, and a governance-approved publisher. Programs read prices only through the registry, never from a single feed directly — so the price source is a plug-in, and feeds can eventually be combined across multiple publishers (a median) instead of trusting one.

Three defenses limit how much damage one bad price can do:

  • Confidence discount. Every payout calculation uses the low end of the confidence band, \hat p_a = p_a - \sigma_a (price minus uncertainty) — so if a price is uncertain, that uncertainty is charged against the payout, not against the pool. Feeds where the uncertainty is too large relative to the price (above a threshold \kappa = 0.96) are excluded outright.
  • Staleness. A price that hasn't updated recently enough is excluded from building new tables. Prices are read once, when a table is built, and then frozen — never re-checked later at settlement or when a player claims a prize.
  • Circuit breaker. A price jump bigger than a set threshold doesn't take effect immediately. It's held in a pending state and only commits if a second, confirming update arrives at least 600 seconds later. One bad or malicious price push can't instantly reprice anything.

At launch, the oracle trusts its one publisher — but what that trust can cost is capped: because the bankroll never holds any card whose value depends on the oracle (§8, S4), a bad price only affects the payout side of a single draw. It never sits on the balance sheet as a liability.

6. The gacha engine#

The core property, and the thing no competitor offers: the odds are calculated on-chain from live inventory and live prices, and locked into the ticket before the random draw happens.

6.1 The odds solver#

Tickets are sold at standing price points — a ticket price c is a parameter, not a hardcoded pack size. Given c, the current available cards (already screened by an allowlist and a price-confidence check), and their current discounted prices \hat v_j, a permissionless crank builds a draw table: cards are sorted into value buckets relative to c (consolation, par, premium, jackpot), and the solver picks bucket probabilities q so that:

\left| \sum_i q_i v_i - \frac{\rho}{10^4}\, c \right| \le \varepsilon\, c, \qquad \sum_i q_i = 1,
q_{\text{jackpot}} \le 5\%, \qquad q_{\text{premium}} \le 30\%, \qquad q_{\text{par}} \le 50\%, \qquad v_{\text{consolation}} \ge \frac{f}{10^4}\, c,

with an RTP target \rho = 92.5\% (written as 9,250 basis points — "bps" is the standard way to express fractions of a percent; 100 bps = 1%), a tolerance \varepsilon = 0.5\%, and a refund floor f = 13\% of the ticket price. In plain terms: whatever value gap is left after the other buckets is filled by a consolation bucket, so a miss always pays back at least 13% of what you paid — in cash by default. If the floor, the RTP target, and the probability caps can't all be satisfied with what's currently in inventory, the solver simply refuses to publish that table rather than publish one it can't honor. Because this math is done with rounding that always favors the floor (never rounds it down), "expected payout stays at or under the RTP target" holds at every price point, not just as a tuned average.

Each price point only admits cards whose price falls between half and 20 times the ticket price ([0.5c, 20c]), checked before the solver runs, with an explicit rejection if a card doesn't qualify — never a silent skip. This keeps the roughly-20x jackpot ceiling something a player can verify themselves, and it stops the protocol from paying to run a solve that inventory can't support anyway.

The floor is a dial, not a fixed number. With the probability of a miss at roughly 73% under current bucket caps, a floor f commits a fixed slice of the ticket's expected payout to misses, leaving the rest for cards:

f_{\max} \approx \frac{\text{EV share paid to misses}}{q_m}.

The default 13% floor corresponds to about 10% of the ticket's expected value being paid out as guaranteed cash — a way of saying the game is roughly 90% about cards, 10% about the safety net. A higher floor is solvable, but it reshapes the game rather than making it more generous overall: money paid out on a miss round-trips right back on the next miss, so a 50% floor doubles the effective edge on money actually at risk while shrinking every card bucket by about 45%. The floor could also be set adaptively per table — solve for the highest floor the current inventory supports, publish that, and promise only "at least f, often more."

Card-guaranteed tables. The protocol's design supports tables where the consolation bucket pays out in cards instead of cash, so every draw returns a card rather than sometimes just money. This is a configuration direction, not something genesis turns on: genesis launches with cash floors, and card-guaranteed tables are added once bulk card inventory is deep enough to support them. The mechanics, if enabled: for a bulk floor value b (the price of the cheapest listable card, working assumption $20), a miss would deliver n = \lfloor f c / b \rfloor cards plus fc - nb in cash, clearing the same floor a cash-only payout would. The floor's size doesn't change either way — the consolation bucket spends the same slice of expected payout whether it leaves as cash or as cards, so this doesn't make any table harder to build. What it needs is enough bulk depth: a table would only guarantee a card on tickets priced above roughly b/f — at $20 bulk cards and a 13% floor, that's about $155. Below that, the floor would still pay in cash. If turned on, this could scale to multiple cards on higher-priced tickets — a $500 ticket's $65 floor could be three bulk cards and $5 cash, instead of hunting for one $65 card the shelf may not stock.

The solved table — every bucket, every probability, the expected value, and the identity of every card in it — is written to an on-chain account and published as an event. Because a live table locks its cards exclusively to itself, this isn't a rarity tier or marketing art: it's this exact card, in this slot, at this probability, visible before anyone spends a dollar. A table stays purchasable only while fresh, so stale prices or inventory force a rebuild instead of quietly drifting — and whatever gets frozen into your ticket at purchase is locked in regardless of what happens afterward.

Price points. The solver works at any ticket price inside the allowed bounds, but every active price point ties up real inventory in its own table. Genesis launches with a single standing price point — $100 — with a deep table behind it, so the first version of the game has one clear, well-stocked way to play rather than a thin book spread across many prices. Additional price points are a configurable follow-on: more get added as inventory and demand justify them. At the $100 tier, the admission range described above works out to $50–$2,000 per card — half the ticket price to twenty times it.

Table size is a per-table limit, not a shelf-wide cap. A single table can carry at most twelve cards, because the crank has to pass each card and its price through one transaction, and twelve is what fits under Solana's legacy transaction-size limit (raising this later is possible with lookup tables). There's no cap at all on how many tables can exist at once — a table's address is derived from the engine, the price point, and the slot it was built in, so any number of them can run side by side. One rule ties it together: a card can only be locked into one live table at a time. What actually limits the shelf size is the cost of running cranks, not this constant — every live table needs periodic rebuilding, and the tip that pays for that comes out of the first ticket sold against it, so a table nobody buys from pays its builder nothing. The shelf grows with demand.

That should be read as a property the design allows for, not a market that already exists. In practice, at genesis the operator will crank its own tables and mostly pay the tip to itself — sized to cover the transaction cost, not to support an independent business yet. What being permissionless buys, even before any outside cranker shows up, is a credible exit: because anyone can build, settle, or close a table and get paid for it, an operator who stopped running cranks couldn't strand the shelf, and no player needs the operator's cooperation to resolve a ticket they've already paid for. A real market of independent crank operators is something that follows from volume, and volume is the thing not yet proven.

buy_ticket locks in the odds snapshot, escrows the ticket price c, reserves the ticket's worst-case cash payout \ell = \max_i \text{cash}_i against the vault, and submits a randomness request tied to the ticket's own account address — all of this happens before any random result exists. Settlement is strictly first-in-first-out (FIFO — first ticket bought, first ticket settled) in a later slot, so the outcome is fully determined by data that existed before the draw: no later ticket can jump the line, and no one settling tickets gets to choose which one to process next.

Randomness comes from a verifiable on-chain source: a callback-style VRF (verifiable random function — a randomness feed that comes with cryptographic proof it wasn't tampered with). No one, including the party settling the ticket, can know the outcome before it's delivered, and the proof means anyone can check afterward that it wasn't manipulated. A provider such as ORAO is the planned source for this. If the randomness callback never arrives, a permissionless timeout path refunds the ticket after a set number of slots. The worst case for the house is a delay, never a theft: there's no instruction that lets the pool see an unfavorable outcome and decline it, and no private backend sits anywhere in this path — building tables, settling tickets, and closing expired ones are all open, permissionless cranks.

6.3 Settlement paths#

A winner of a card outcome chooses:

  • Keep it. The card transfers out; you own it outright.
  • Sell it back now. An instant buyback at a fraction \lambda of the price the pool just paid the consignor for that same card seconds earlier — scoped to the card you just won, and only for a short window afterward. At \lambda \approx 0.95 and the payout haircut applied on the way in, this returns roughly 89.6% of the card's frozen value, with no wait.
  • List it back on consignment. The shelf is also the exit door: list the card you just won back into the queue (§7.4) at a payout set by rule from the oracle at listing time, and get paid when someone else draws it — typically around 94% of value at the default terms. Slower, but you set the price you're willing to accept. Because the pool pays a price the oracle set at listing and a real draw confirmed, this path needs no trust in the oracle at all.
  • Roll into the house. Same exit as above, but take payment in HOUSE shares instead of cash (§7.6): the proceeds mint shares at NAV, and you become an LP.

Neither exit is priced by the house. Every competitor cashes winners out at a rate the operator publishes and can change — which makes that single number the hinge of the whole trade, deciding what a win was actually worth, with the player having no say in it.

The instant-buyback price is anchored to something the protocol already paid, not to a live guess. When a card is drawn, the pool settles with its consignor at a price P set by consignors competing for shelf space (§7.4), confirmed by an actual paying draw, and recorded on-chain. Buying back at \lambda P reads no oracle at all — that's what keeps it safe. A standing cash offer priced directly off the oracle is the one design that turns an oracle mistake into repeated losses for LPs (measured, not assumed — see VERIFICATION.md); this instant buyback isn't that, because it's scoped to one just-won card for a short window, not a standing quote anyone can lean on. Cards bought back this way also enter the books at zero value (§8, S4), so holding one can never inflate NAV.

The slower consign-back path lets the winner set the price. It's the only exit where you can ask for more than the instant buyback offers — worth doing if you think the card is underpriced.

Cash outcomes (consolation payouts, refunds) pay out of vault liquidity directly.

Claim expiry. Every settled outcome has a claim window (on the order of 180 days). After that, a permissionless crank can close it: unclaimed cash returns to the pool, and an unclaimed card — whose consignor was already paid at settlement — goes to the treasury to be re-listed. Nothing stays open forever; cleaning up old state is a security property, not just housekeeping.

7. Consignment: non-custodial inventory liquidity#

The vault (§4) supplies the balance sheet: cash at risk, shares priced at NAV, exposure to a loss, a share of the profit. Consignment supplies the other half — inventory. A consignor puts a card into escrow rather than selling it. It comes back to them if it's delisted or the table closes unsold, and it only leaves their ownership when a draw actually wins it. Consigning takes on no pool exposure, mints no shares just for listing, and pays out the instant a card is won — fast, certain money.

The two channels complement each other: the bankroll grows with money looking for yield, while consignment keeps cards flowing even when owners don't want to sell outright — a listing costs nothing to hold, and only becomes a sale, at the price the consignor set, when a draw actually claims it. Consignment is the protocol's main inventory channel: a genesis book of just a few dozen listed cards is enough to build the first tables, with no cards owned by the pool itself.

7.1 The three-way trade#

A consignor lists a card. The oracle's price at that moment, discounted for uncertainty (\hat v_{\text{listing}} = p - \sigma), is read and locked in. The consignor's payout is set by a fixed rule: P := \hat v_{\text{listing}} \cdot (1 - h), where h is the consignment haircut (default 5.75%, which folds together the old consignment margin and a 1% settlement fee). Listing requires passing an allowlist and the same price-confidence check used elsewhere.

If a player draws the card and wins: the consignor gets paid P (fast, certain money, fixed at listing time), the player gets a card worth V_f, and the pool's margin is V_f - P. The solver charges that card against the ticket's RTP budget at its discounted oracle value, not at whatever the pool actually paid for it — so this margin flows straight to the pool on top of the ticket's edge, and nothing is subtracted from what the player receives.

A consigned card is never counted toward the pool's value, its exposure limits, or NAV. Instead, the pool reserves a matching cash liability against it.

7.2 Delivery: escrow#

A listed card sits in escrow. The consignor still owns it — it comes back to them if delisted or if a table closes unsold, and it only moves to a winner when an actual draw takes it — but they can't pull it out from under a table that's currently live. Delivery is mechanical, not something that depends on trust: there's nothing to renege on, so no bond is needed. The card is never counted on the pool's own balance sheet at any point (§8, S4).

Genesis inventory consists of Collector Crypt–minted cards (programmable NFTs) whose transfer rules currently block them from sitting in a program-controlled vault. So genesis escrow works through attested treasury custody instead: a disclosed key holds the cards, with an on-chain listing record and the same delist, return, and payment-on-draw rights the program will enforce directly once the card's transfer rules allow an on-chain vault. A wallet-custody alternative — where the card never moves and delivery is guaranteed by a posted bond instead — is specified in Appendix B as a future path.

7.3 A worked example#

A consignor holds a tokenized PSA 9 card the oracle prices at a midpoint of $400 with a confidence width of $16, so the discounted price is \hat v = \$384. This is locked in at listing. The consignor's payout is P = \$384 \times (1 - 0.0575) \approx \$361.92, and the card moves into escrow. A crank slots it into tables by value — at the $100 genesis tier, a $384 card is a premium slot (about four times the ticket price). Until a table locks it in, the listing costs nothing to hold and can be pulled at any time.

A $100 ticket draws it. The consignor receives $361.92. The player, who paid $100, receives a card the oracle values at $400. The pool paid $361.92 for roughly $400 of delivered prize value, and that margin flows into NAV.

7.4 The listing queue#

Listings that pass the screening gate enter a waiting queue rather than going straight into a table. The table-building crank fills each value bucket from the front of the queue, strictly by listing time — first listed, first drawn. Because selection is a pure sort over on-chain data, the crank has no discretion over whose card gets shelf space, and queue position never changes the odds — those are set per table regardless of arrival order. Because payouts are locked in from the oracle at listing time with no price competition between consignors, first-listed-first-drawn is the natural order. When a settlement uses up an item, the next rebuild pulls the next one off the front of the queue, so tables restock automatically.

One gate sits above the queue: a per-set exposure cap. The share of a table's total expected payout that comes from any one card set can't exceed a set limit of the pool's NAV. A card that would breach this is explicitly declined and stays queued — never silently skipped. This is an admission gate, not a solvency guarantee: NAV moves after every table build, so the cap limits what the shelf can admit at that moment, not a certified permanent bound. The default limit is set from measured inventory concentration, not chosen arbitrarily.

How fast a card clears the queue depends on its bucket, but less dramatically than the bucket caps alone suggest. Within a bucket, the draw is uniform, so one card's own odds are the bucket's probability divided by the number of cards sharing that bucket: with a roughly 73% miss rate spread across six par-bucket item slots, a given bulk card clears about once every eight tickets, while a lone jackpot card at the 5% cap clears once every twenty tickets, or one of three jackpot cards every sixty. That's two to seven times slower, not a different order of magnitude.

What actually slows down high-value inventory is the payout budget and the pool's size cap, not the bucket cap itself. A $5,000 card can't sit in the 5%-probability jackpot slot on a $100 ticket, because that alone would cost $250 against a payout budget of only $92.50 — it would have to enter at closer to 1%, eating half the budget, clearing roughly once every hundred tickets. Below a certain pool size, it can't enter at all, because the per-ticket payout cap (§8, S3) won't allow a single payout that large. What limits the shelf's high end is bankroll size, and that grows with deposits, not with a parameter change. Below that ceiling, the pool's steadiest demand is for exactly the low-value inventory that has no other liquid market — bulk cards priced too low for custodial marketplaces to bother with, or unprofitable once shipping and fees are factored in. A card the pool has bought back from a winner (§6.3) re-enters as a fresh listing at the back of the queue, at a payout no higher than what the original consignor received — recycled inventory restocks the shelf without jumping ahead of new consignments.

7.5 Advance on listing (extension)#

Because listings sit in escrow, the pool could advance a consignor a fraction (up to 70%) of their payout P right at listing time, instead of making them wait for a draw. The advance would be repaid out of the draw payout (the consignor receives the remainder on draw) or repaid in full to delist and get the card back — structurally a secured loan, collateralized by the card at its listing-time oracle price, at roughly 1.5x coverage under the stated bounds. Total advances outstanding would be capped as a fraction of liquid USDC, so they can never compete with cash locked against open tickets (§8, S2).

What this buys is timing, not more shelf capacity. It would let a consignor get cash at listing time instead of waiting for a draw. It doesn't change what the shelf can carry — that's set by the pool's capacity cap, which only grows with more bankroll.

7.6 Pay-in-shares: consign your way into the house#

At listing, a consignor sets one flag: on draw, pay me in USDC — or pay me in HOUSE shares, minted at that moment's NAV. Nothing else about the process changes; settlement mints shares instead of transferring cash, using the same payout P. Shares minted this way go straight into the same withdrawal cooldown as any other LP — so this flag can never be used to skip the cooldown. This is the safe version of "deposit a card, become the house": the card's price was set by queue competition and confirmed by a real paying draw before any shares are minted, so the mint happens on realized cash, never a guess about the card's value. Consignors who leave this flag on gradually convert from card suppliers into shareholders — with no reward token or emissions program needed to make that happen.

8. Solvency#

The pool's solvency rests on several layered protections rather than any one defense. Roughly ordered from per-ticket to pool-wide:

S1 — Worst-case reservation. Every open ticket locks up its maximum possible cash payout, not the average one: \ell = \max_i \text{cash}_i, reserved the moment it's bought and released exactly once when the ticket resolves. Every bucket has a cash value attached, including the card buckets — if earlier draws already used up a bucket's cards, the payout falls back to that bucket's average cash value instead. (An exception is any future card-guaranteed table, §6.1, where the card bucket would be reserved in kind rather than in cash — a design direction, not something genesis ships.) This reservation covers every path a ticket can take, so the pool never carries an open bet it might not be able to pay.

S2 — Locked money never exceeds liquid money. The one instruction that increases locked cash L enforces:

\sum_{\text{open tickets}} \ell_t \;\le\; L \;\le\; C.

Cards or other illiquid assets can never back a cash promise. Money owed on outstanding instant buybacks (§6.3) is capped the same way, as a fraction of liquid USDC, so the fast-exit path can never crowd out money reserved for open tickets.

S3 — Size caps modeled on the Kelly criterion. Total open exposure and the largest single ticket price are both capped as fractions of the pool's value. The house can never take a bet large enough to threaten its own survival, so bad luck can't bankrupt a solvent pool.

Read the ticket-price cap carefully — it limits the ticket price, not the payout, and the gap between those two is the real risk. What actually bounds a single draw's cost is the largest multiple of the ticket price the solver can construct, which comes from the bucket caps: a jackpot bucket sitting at its 5% probability cap, spending the entire 92.5% payout budget on that one bucket, pays out 92.5% ÷ 5% = 18.5 times the ticket price. So work it through: at the reference cap, a ticket can be at most 1% of pool value (NAV). A single draw can therefore cost 18.5 times that ticket price — 18.5 × 1% of NAV = 18.5% of NAV in one payout, not 1%. That's the number worth remembering, and it's why the ticket-price cap sits two orders of magnitude below what a single draw can actually cost. This is a deliberate tradeoff, not an oversight: it's what lets even a small pool offer a prize worth playing for, and §9.1 works out what recovering from one of these costs in time.

S4 — Nothing on the balance sheet depends on an oracle price. The vault's reserves are USDC, full stop — the pool never takes ownership of a card at a price an oracle supplied. Cards bought back from winners (§6.3) are the one exception to "no cards," but not an exception to the rule that matters: they're booked at zero value against a price the pool itself just paid, so they can't inflate NAV, can't be used to back a withdrawal, and can't be drained through a bad price — they're pure upside once they're re-listed. All payout math runs on the discounted price and rejects feeds with too much uncertainty, so an oracle mistake is charged to one draw's payout, bounded by that ticket's own value, rather than sitting on the balance sheet.

S5 — The solver refuses cleanly. If the RTP target, refund floor, and bucket caps can't all be satisfied with current inventory, no table gets published. The protocol would rather not sell a ticket than sell one it might not be able to honor.

S6 — Withdrawal discipline. The 7-day cooldown, plus the rule that withdrawals can only draw against C - L, prevent both jackpot-timing exits and draining reserves out from under a bet that hasn't resolved yet.

S7 — Consignment isolation. Consigned cards are never added into the pool's value; the cash owed against them is fully reserved, and the card backing that liability sits in escrow for as long as the table is live. Because every listed card reserves its own payout obligation, the live shelf is bounded by bankroll size — inventory capacity only grows as LP capital does.

S8 — Mispricing is bounded and frozen in place. The oracle price is read once at listing time, the consignor's payout is fixed by rule from that price, and the consignor bears the haircut either way. A player picks their own ticket price against a table that's already published. The oracle's own value is the one price nobody in the trade gets to choose, so its damage is structurally limited: cards enter the odds math at the discounted price, freeze into the table at build time and into the ticket at settlement, and are never re-read when a prize is claimed — no payout drifts because of claim timing or a later price move. A bad price from the publisher could still skew the odds of tables built after that bad update lands; the confidence discount, staleness rule, admission threshold, and the 600-second staged circuit breaker (§5) all limit how far that can go. And because the instant buyback is anchored to the frozen payout P from the original listing-time price — not a live re-read (§6.3) — a bad price costs a single draw instead of compounding into repeated losses for LPs.

S9 — Bulk reservation on card-guaranteed tables (design direction). If card-guaranteed tables (§6.1) are turned on, each would reserve enough bulk cards to cover every open ticket sold against it, and would stop selling if bulk inventory ever couldn't keep up. This would be the one place the protocol owes inventory rather than cash — a different kind of obligation from everything else on this list, bounded by refusing to take on more exposure rather than by cash reservation. Genesis does not enable this configuration.

Solvency and yield are separate claims: the invariants above bound insolvency — stress-tested across a full range of scenarios, see VERIFICATION.md — but they don't promise that LP returns stay attractive through a downturn.

Two mechanisms fund shareholder yield, both paid for out of money players and consignors actually spend:

\underbrace{(1 - \rho)\, c}_{\text{house edge per ticket}} \;+\; \underbrace{(V_f - P)}_{\text{consignment discount}} \;\longrightarrow\; \nu \uparrow

1. Player edge. Every ticket pays c, and the table returns \rho \cdot c on average. That margin is whatever the solver — checked bit-for-bit against an independent reference implementation — actually computes, not a number the house asserts after the fact. A small protocol fee (0.25%, plus an oracle fee) is carved out per ticket; the rest flows into NAV. 2. Consignment discount. The gap between what a card is worth (V_f) and what the consignor was paid (P) — directly observable per listing, and funded by consignors' own preference for fast, certain cash (§7.1). It's what lets the pool run a real profit margin while players still receive close to full value in prizes.

When players win, NAV falls a little; over many draws the edge favors the pool — similar in shape to how GLP or Gamba's liquidity pools work, but with a tokenized trading-card gacha layered on top, and odds you can verify yourself.

9.1 Yield is turnover times edge#

An LP holding cash has no upfront cost to earn back, so gross annual return is roughly:

\text{APR} \approx e \cdot T,

where e \approx 7.0\% is the per-ticket LP take at launch fee settings (§9.2), and T is how many times the pool's entire NAV gets turned over in ticket sales in a year. This only counts the ticket edge. The consignment discount (V_f - P, §7.1) adds to this on top, and is deliberately left out of the headline number here — it depends on how hard consignors compete for shelf space, which hasn't been measured yet, and a headline return shouldn't lean on an unmeasured assumption. Every yield figure in this paper is the ticket edge alone; the consignment spread is upside not counted.

Flip the formula around and it tells you how much of a stake offsets a habit: a player spending v a year on tickets pays about 7.5% of that in structural margin, and a deposit D earns D \cdot e \cdot T — the two cancel out at

\frac{D}{v} \approx \frac{1.07}{T}.

That relationship drives §10.3 below. The bankroll earns on flow, not stock — the same dollar gets re-bet against many tickets a year — and the relationship holds at any pool size: hitting a 50% APR needs turnover T \approx 7\times whether the pool is $1,000 (clearing two $10 tickets a day) or $100,000 (clearing $2,000 a day). The real constraint isn't the formula, it's bootstrapping: the size caps (S3) mean a small pool can only offer small prizes, which limits the volume the formula depends on — so bankroll and shelf inventory have to grow together. Drawdowns are real: a jackpot early in a pool's life can put NAV underwater for a while, until the edge grinds it back up.

How long "grinds it back" actually takes. A payout worth k times the ticket price costs the pool (k - \rho)c more than the \rho c it budgeted for, and the edge earns e \cdot c per ticket sold afterward. So the number of tickets needed to recover from a k\times hit is:

N \approx \frac{k - \rho}{e},

and this holds at any scale — the ticket price c cancels out, so the answer depends only on the multiple and the edge, never on how big the ticket or the pool is. A $25 pool and a $25,000 pool recover from their respective jackpots in the same number of draws. Plugging in the S3 ceiling (k = 18.5, \rho = 92.5\%) and the LP take of about 7.0% gives roughly 251 tickets. Since the reference ticket-price cap puts a single ticket at 1% of NAV, those 251 tickets add up to about 2.5x NAV in total sales. At the turnover level (T \approx 7) that a 50% APR assumes, the worst single draw the protocol allows takes a little over four months to recover from. (Use the LP take here, not the full 7.5% player-side margin — the protocol fee is skimmed off before NAV sees it, so the pool climbs out at the LP's rate, not the gross rate.)

Two things follow from this. Recovery is a volume problem, not a capital one — an underwater pool doesn't need a rescue deposit, it needs more tickets sold, and nothing about being underwater stops it from selling. And the real question for an LP isn't "can a jackpot bankrupt the pool" (S1–S3 already answer that: no) but "can a jackpot outlast my patience" — which is really a question about whether turnover T holds up during the drawdown.

At the 3% bound, the treasury would collect roughly 3% of ticket volume plus roughly 0.8% of volume through consignment settlements; at launch settings, nearly the whole edge flows to LPs instead, prioritizing bankroll growth over treasury revenue early on.

10. Player mechanics#

One rule governs this section: player rewards are funded from dedicated fee lines, never from the RTP budget or from reserves. Every mechanic below is stateless and solved in advance, with its odds and obligations published in on-chain state before you play. These mechanics themselves aren't new to the industry; publishing them with proof instead of asking players to trust a server is.

10.1 Bundles: guarantees priced in advance#

Bundles are designed here and planned for a later release, not part of the genesis launch. A bundle would be N draws sold as a single ticket at price N \cdot c, resolved by one randomness request, with a guarantee: at least K of the N outcomes are cards (or better). It reuses existing machinery: K slots would draw from card-only tables (no cash bucket — workable whenever the cheapest listable card fits the slot's budget), the remaining N-K slots draw from standard tables, and the randomness result would shuffle which slot is which, so no fixed slot always carries the guarantee.

The guarantee would be paid for inside the same RTP budget, not created for free: bundle expected value stays at \rho \cdot N \cdot c, so certainty at the bottom costs a slightly leaner top end — the guaranteed slots skew toward bulk cards, and the jackpot bucket shrinks to make room. The effect is meaningful: four independent $25 draws leave about a 28% chance of getting zero cards; a bundle guaranteeing at least 2 cards would make that 0%.

This is the honest version of the industry's "pity" mechanic. A typical pity counter lives on a server you can't inspect, tracked across your whole play history. A bundle guarantee would be a constraint written into a published table, provable before you buy. It's also deliberately scoped: a guarantee holds within one bundle and doesn't carry over between bundles. Bundles would also settle on a single randomness request (spreading that cost across N draws) and use up multiple inventory items per purchase, moving the queue along faster.

10.2 Volatility flavors#

Also designed here and planned for a later release, not part of genesis. The idea: two tables per active price point, same price, same RTP target, different shape — the refund-floor dial (§6.1) exposed directly as a player choice:

10.3 Playing the house you are playing against#

The pool's edge is paid by whoever buys tickets and collected by whoever holds shares. Nothing stops those from being the same person. A player who also holds shares is on both sides of the table: they pay roughly 7.5% margin on their own tickets, and collect roughly 7.0% of the margin on everyone's tickets, including strangers'.

That second part is the whole point. This isn't your own losses being recycled back to you — it's funded by pool-wide turnover (§9.1), which is dominated by other people's play. Your own tickets are a rounding error in that total. Setting the yearly margin you pay equal to the yearly yield your shares earn tells you how big a position neutralizes your own habit:

Pool turnover TShares needed, as a share of your annual ticket spend
2×~54%
4×~27%

12×~9%

At T = 7 — a $1,000 pool clearing two $10 tickets a day — a player who spends $1,000 a year on tickets and holds $150 of shares expects to roughly break even against the house edge, while still taking on the full variance of whatever they draw.

This hedge gets stronger exactly where the game gets more expensive. A player's other cost, beyond the edge, is the spread they give up consigning a won card back — and that spread widens exactly when the listing queue is deep (§7.4). The same depth that thins out your own exit also fattens the pool's margin on every card it moves, and that lands in NAV. A shareholder is short their own exit spread but long everyone else's — the exit haircut is paid on the cards you happen to win, and collected on every card the shelf turns over. This ratio doesn't depend on pool size, only on how hard the pool's money works: it improves as volume grows relative to bankroll, and weakens if bankroll outruns volume. It's an expectation, not a guarantee — a jackpot paid out of NAV is a real loss on the share side too.

This isn't a stock buyback. A buyback is a choice: a company decides to bid, at a price it sets, in a market where the money can leak to sellers who aren't you. Edge accrual here is automatic — the margin on every settled ticket lands in the vault without anyone deciding to make it happen, nothing leaks out, and the result is computable on-chain at any moment as V/S. It's the difference between a company promising to support its own price, and a balance sheet that simply grows.

Streak bonuses, daily rolls, volume tiers, and sponsored overlays are treasury policy, not protocol rules — funded from the protocol fee (§9.2) and changeable without touching a program (Appendix B).

Appendix B: deferred mechanisms#

Specified in earlier drafts and companion documents; not part of the base protocol at genesis:

  • Direct card deposits into the bankroll — removed from v1 scope entirely (not merely superseded). Earlier plans included oracle pricing, haircut-credited share minting, and a regime-scaled ladder of caps (calm / bear / crisis markets), but consignment ships without any of that automation. Consign-then-pay-in-shares (§7.6) is the only path into card equity at launch.
  • Consignor-held custody with a delivery bond — the card never leaves the owner's wallet, and delivery is guaranteed economically instead of by escrow. Before listing, the consignor would post a bond B = \max\big( (V_f - P)(1 + m),\ \phi V_f \big), with margin m decaying from 50% toward 25% with a clean history, and a floor \phi = 10\%. If the consignor reneges, the winner receives the bond, topped up by the pool to the card's full value V_f; since B \ge V_f - P by construction, the pool's own added cost is capped at P — never more than honest delivery would have cost. Modeling on real price history suggests reneging only becomes tempting past a 2–4 week listing window, so this path would carry a listing-duration cap, bond re-checks at table build time, and an automatic top-up-or-delist crank. Superseded at genesis by direct escrow (§7.2).
  • Haircut-rebate vesting — only meaningful alongside direct card deposits; deferred along with them.
  • Per-tier oracle circuit breakers — a single conservative global breaker ships first.
  • Oracle-priced standing buyback — a continuous pool bid on any eligible card at a fraction of its oracle value. This stays deferred: a standing bid priced directly off the oracle is exactly the design that turns a price mistake into repeated losses for LPs. The instant buyback that did ship (§6.3) is a different thing — priced off a real consignment sale, scoped to a just-won card, and time-limited — and that reasoning doesn't apply to it.
  • Advance on listing (§7.5) — designed, gated on custodied listings becoming available.
  • Sponsored overlays — third parties could fund bonus prize buckets on tables at published odds. Marketing budgets as provable player value.
  • Retention policy — streak bonuses, daily rolls, volume tiers, and leaderboard prizes: treasury programs funded from the protocol fee, never from the RTP budget or reserves, changeable without a program upgrade.
  • Referrer / distribution fee — a per-ticket fee for third-party frontends that bring in players is a candidate future addition, tracked separately, and not part of genesis.
