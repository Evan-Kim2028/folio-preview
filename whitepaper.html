Folio Club: A Protocol-Owned Gacha House for Tokenized Trading Cards

Abstract#

You pay $100 for a ticket. Before you buy, you see the full odds — every card you could win, its probability, its value — frozen on-chain so they cannot change after you commit. Miss, and you still get at least $13 back. No one, including the operators, can see a result before the draw.

Every card gacha shipped so far has a company running the house: private odds, private randomness, all of the profit. Folio Club replaces the company with a pool anyone can join — deposit USDC and earn the house's edge, or consign a card into escrow and get paid the moment a player draws it. Profits accrue to the value of a pool share; there is no reward token and no emissions.

1. Introduction#

Running the house — the side of the bet that wins on average — is the profitable side of any gacha game, and every platform so far reserves that seat for a company. The category sold over a billion dollars of packs in eighteen months; Collector Crypt alone booked $146.9M in Q1 2026, about 98% of it from gacha. The cards, the storage, and the settlement are all tokenized — the one position that reliably makes money is still closed.

It stays closed because nobody outside the operator can verify the house's edge. RipIndex, an independent on-chain tracker, reverse-engineers what platforms don't publish: Collector Crypt realizes −5.4% for players across 923,587 draws, with machines ranging from −21.7% to +10.6%; GachaPull advertises +2.0% and delivers −5.1%; Courtyard delivers −17.9%. These are careful estimates of numbers every platform could simply have published — and none does.

Folio Club makes the odds a property anyone can check instead of a claim to trust: 92.5% ± 0.5% return-to-play on every table, a 13% floor on every miss, frozen on-chain before the draw exists (§2). Nothing to reverse-engineer, nothing for the house to refuse — which is what makes it safe to pool outside money into a shared bankroll (§4). The one launch-time trust is the price oracle's single disclosed publisher (§6): this house can misjudge what a prize is worth; it cannot choose who wins.

Three predecessors shaped the design. FWA proved a permissionless house works, but let depositors price their own collateral and paid yield in emissions. Collector Crypt proved the product at scale, but kept the house closed. OlympusDAO proved protocol-owned liquidity, but wrapped it in a reflexive token. Folio Club keeps the product, the open house, and the owned bankroll — with oracle prices instead of self-reported values, real profit instead of emissions, and odds on-chain instead of on a private server.

2. The game#

Odds are calculated on-chain from live inventory and live prices, and locked into the ticket before the random draw happens.

2.1 The odds solver#

Tickets are sold at standing price points — a ticket price c is a parameter, not a hardcoded pack size. Given c, the current available cards (already screened by an allowlist and a price-confidence check), and their current discounted prices \hat v_j, a permissionless crank builds a draw table: cards are sorted into value buckets relative to c (consolation, par, premium, jackpot), and the solver picks bucket probabilities q so that:

\left| \sum_i q_i v_i - \frac{\rho}{10^4}\, c \right| \le \varepsilon\, c, \qquad \sum_i q_i = 1,
q_{\text{jackpot}} \le 5\%, \qquad q_{\text{premium}} \le 30\%, \qquad q_{\text{par}} \le 50\%, \qquad v_{\text{consolation}} \ge \frac{f}{10^4}\, c,

with RTP target \rho = 92.5\% (9,250 bps), tolerance \varepsilon = 0.5\%, and refund floor f = 13\%. A miss always pays back at least 13% of what you paid, in cash by default. If these can't all be satisfied with current inventory, the solver refuses to publish. Rounding always favors the floor.

Each price point admits only cards priced [0.5c, 20c], checked before the solver runs, with explicit rejection.

The floor is a dial. At the roughly 73% miss rate under current caps, the default 13% floor is about 10% of expected value — roughly 90% cards, 10% safety net. A 50% floor is solvable but doubles the effective edge on money at risk while shrinking every card bucket by about 45%.

Card-guaranteed tables — consolation paid in cards, so every draw returns a card — are a configurable future direction once bulk inventory is deep enough, not genesis. Details in Appendix B.

The solved table — every bucket, probability, expected value, card identity — is written on-chain and published before anyone spends a dollar, and frozen regardless of what happens afterward.

Price points. Genesis launches with one tier — $100 — with a deep table, rather than a thin book across many prices; admission range $50–$2,000 per card. More tiers are a configurable follow-on.

Table size. A table carries at most twelve cards (Solana transaction limit; raisable later). What actually limits shelf size is crank cost: every live table needs periodic rebuilding, paid by a tip from the first ticket sold against it. Cranks (permissionless bots that build, settle, and close tables for a tip) are open to anyone — uncapturable, though still dependent on volume not yet proven.

buy_ticket locks in the odds snapshot, escrows c, reserves the ticket's worst-case cash payout \ell = \max_i \text{cash}_i, and submits a randomness request — all before any result exists. Settlement is strictly FIFO in a later slot.

Randomness comes from a callback-style VRF (verifiable random function, cryptographically proven untampered); a provider such as ORAO is the planned source. If the callback never arrives, a permissionless timeout refunds the ticket. Worst case for the house is a delay, never a theft — no instruction lets the pool decline an unfavorable outcome, and no private backend sits anywhere in this path.

2.3 Settlement paths#

A winner chooses:

  • Keep it. The card transfers out.
  • Sell it back now. Instant buyback at \lambda \approx 0.95 of the price the pool just paid the consignor — scoped to the just-won card, short window. Returns roughly 89.6% of frozen value, no wait.
  • List it back on consignment. Back into the queue (§3.4) at a payout set from the oracle at listing time — typically ~94% of value. Slower, but you set the price.
  • Roll into the house. Same, but in HOUSE shares (§3.6): proceeds mint shares at NAV, and you become an LP.

Neither exit is priced by the house — every competitor cashes winners out at a rate it publishes and can change.

The instant-buyback price is anchored to what the protocol already paid: settled with the consignor at P set by queue competition (§3.4), confirmed by a real paying draw. Buying back at \lambda P reads no oracle — unlike a standing bid priced directly off the oracle (see VERIFICATION.md), this is scoped to one just-won card, time-limited. Cards bought back this way enter the books at zero value (§5, S4), so holding one can never inflate NAV.

Claim expiry. Every settled outcome has a claim window (~180 days). After that, a permissionless crank closes it: unclaimed cash returns to the pool, an unclaimed card (consignor already paid) goes to treasury for re-listing.

3. Consignment: non-custodial inventory liquidity#

The vault (§4) supplies the balance sheet. Consignment supplies the inventory. A consignor puts a card into escrow rather than selling it outright: it comes back if delisted or the table closes unsold, and only leaves their ownership when a draw wins it. Consigning takes on no pool exposure, mints no shares just for listing, and pays out the instant a card is won.

Consignment is the protocol's main inventory channel — a genesis book of a few dozen listed cards is enough to build the first tables, with no cards owned by the pool itself.

3.1 The three-way trade#

A consignor lists a card; the oracle price, discounted for uncertainty (\hat v_{\text{listing}} = p - \sigma), is locked in. Payout is fixed by rule: P := \hat v_{\text{listing}} \cdot (1 - h), h the consignment haircut (default 5.75%, margin plus a 1% settlement fee). Listing requires the same allowlist and price-confidence check used elsewhere.

If drawn: the consignor gets P, the player gets a card worth V_f, and the pool's margin is V_f - P — charged against the ticket's RTP budget at discounted oracle value, so nothing is subtracted from what the player receives.

A consigned card is never counted toward pool value, exposure limits, or NAV; the pool reserves a matching cash liability against it.

3.2 Delivery: escrow#

A listed card sits in escrow, still owned by the consignor, but can't be pulled out from under a live table. Delivery is mechanical — nothing to renege on, so no bond is needed (§5, S4).

Genesis inventory is Collector Crypt–minted cards (programmable NFTs) whose transfer rules currently block program-controlled custody. So genesis escrow runs through attested treasury custody instead: a disclosed key holds the cards, with the same delist, return, and payment-on-draw rights the program will enforce directly once transfer rules allow it. A wallet-custody alternative (bond-guaranteed delivery, card never moves) is in Appendix B.

3.3 A worked example#

A PSA 9 card is oracle-priced at $400 midpoint, $16 confidence width: discounted price \hat v = \$384. Payout P = \$384 \times (1 - 0.0575) \approx \$361.92. At the $100 genesis tier, the card lands in the premium bucket (~4× ticket price).

A $100 ticket draws it: the consignor gets $361.92, the player gets a $400 card, and the pool's $361.92-for-$400 margin flows into NAV.

3.4 The listing queue#

Listings that pass screening enter a waiting queue rather than going straight into a table. The table-building crank fills each value bucket strictly by listing time — first listed, first drawn — a pure sort with no crank discretion; queue position never changes the odds. When a settlement uses an item, the next rebuild pulls the next one off the front.

One gate sits above the queue: a per-set exposure cap — no card set can exceed a set share of a table's total expected payout. A card that would breach this is declined and stays queued, never silently skipped.

Bucket position changes clearing speed by roughly 2–7×, not orders of magnitude (a bulk par-bucket card clears about once every eight tickets; a lone jackpot card once every twenty). The high end of what the shelf can carry is capped by bankroll size: a $5,000 card can't fit a 5% jackpot slot on a $100 ticket without eating half the payout budget, so it clears slower below a certain pool size. The low end — bulk cards with no other liquid market — is the pool's steadiest demand. A card bought back from a winner (§2.3) re-enters at the back of the queue, at a payout no higher than the original consignor received.

3.5 Pay-in-shares: consign your way into the house#

At listing, a consignor sets one flag: pay me in USDC on draw, or in HOUSE shares minted at that moment's NAV, using the same payout P. Shares minted this way go into the same withdrawal cooldown as any other LP. The mint happens on realized cash, not a guess, since P was already set by queue competition and confirmed by a real paying draw — how a consignor gradually converts from card supplier into shareholder, no reward token needed.

4. The house#

The vault holds USDC. Its financial state is two numbers: cash on hand (C), and cash locked against open bets (L).

4.1 Shares and NAV#

When you deposit, you get shares priced at the pool's net asset value (NAV) — pool value divided by shares outstanding. A deposit of d USDC mints

\Delta S = \frac{d \cdot S}{V}

shares (first deposit mints 1:1), and withdrawing \Delta S shares redeems \Delta S \cdot \nu USDC at current NAV. Pool value is read from numbers the program itself tracks, never a live wallet balance — so no one can inflate NAV by donating tokens directly to the vault (the "ERC-4626 inflation attack").

There is no separate reward token. A HOUSE share is a direct claim on the vault's assets — profit shows up as rising NAV per share.

4.2 Withdrawals#

Withdrawing is two steps: request, then wait out a cooldown (genesis: 7 days), then execute at current NAV. The cooldown stops timing a withdrawal to dodge an incoming jackpot payout, and can only draw against C - L.

Two mechanisms fund shareholder yield, both paid for out of money players and consignors actually spend:

\underbrace{(1 - \rho)\, c}_{\text{house edge per ticket}} \;+\; \underbrace{(V_f - P)}_{\text{consignment discount}} \;\longrightarrow\; \nu \uparrow
  1. Player edge. Every ticket pays c; the table returns \rho \cdot c on average. A small protocol fee (0.25%, plus an oracle fee) is carved out per ticket; the rest flows into NAV.
  2. Consignment discount. The gap between a card's worth (V_f) and what the consignor was paid (P, §3.1), funded by consignors' own preference for fast, certain cash.

Similar in shape to how GLP or Gamba's liquidity pools work, but with a tokenized trading-card gacha layered on top, and odds you can verify yourself.

4.4 Yield is turnover times edge#

Gross annual return is roughly:

\text{APR} \approx e \cdot T,

where e \approx 7.0\% is the per-ticket LP take at launch fee settings (§4.5), and T is annual NAV turnover in ticket sales. Ticket edge only — the consignment discount adds on top but is left out since it depends on unmeasured consignor competition.

The bankroll earns on flow, not stock: 50% APR needs T \approx 7\times at any pool size. The constraint is bootstrapping — size caps (§5, S3) mean a small pool can only offer small prizes, limiting the volume the formula depends on.

Recovery from a jackpot. A payout worth k times the ticket price costs the pool (k - \rho)c more than budgeted, recovered at e \cdot c per ticket sold afterward:

N \approx \frac{k - \rho}{e}

tickets — independent of ticket price or pool size. At the S3 ceiling (k = 18.5, \rho = 92.5\%, e \approx 7.0\%), that's roughly 251 tickets (~2.5× NAV in sales), a little over four months at T \approx 7. This is a volume problem, not a capital one — no rescue deposit needed.

A player who also holds shares pays ~7.5% margin on their own tickets and collects ~7.0% on everyone's — dominated by other people's play. At T=7, roughly 15% of annual spend held as shares offsets the edge paid. This isn't discretionary: the margin on every settled ticket lands automatically, computable on-chain as V/S.

Fees split the house's edge; they never come out of the player's side of the trade. Per ticket, the structural margin (~7.5% of ticket price) divides among:

Frontends (apps built on top of the protocol) are permissionless. At genesis there's no separate referrer fee.

The consignment fee leaves a consignor with roughly 89–94% of the card's oracle value — above typical marketplace nets (~87%), paid out far faster. LP deposits and withdrawals are free; the cooldown, not a fee, protects against timed withdrawals.

Treasury fee lines fund retention and product incentives, never the RTP budget or reserves — solvency stays firewalled from marketing spend. At the 3% bound, treasury collects roughly 3% of volume plus ~0.8% through consignment; at launch settings, nearly the whole edge flows to LPs.

5. Solvency#

The pool's solvency rests on layered protections rather than any one defense. Roughly ordered from per-ticket to pool-wide:

S1 — Worst-case reservation. Every open ticket locks up its maximum possible cash payout: \ell = \max_i \text{cash}_i, reserved on purchase and released on resolution. The pool never carries a bet it might not be able to pay.

S2 — Locked money never exceeds liquid money.

\sum_{\text{open tickets}} \ell_t \;\le\; L \;\le\; C.

Cards can never back a cash promise. Instant buybacks are capped the same way, as a fraction of liquid USDC.

S3 — Size caps modeled on the Kelly criterion. Total open exposure and the largest single ticket price are both capped as fractions of pool value. The ticket-price cap limits the ticket price, not the payout: a jackpot bucket at its 5% cap, spending the full 92.5% payout budget, pays 92.5% ÷ 5% = 18.5× the ticket price. At the reference cap (ticket ≤ 1% of NAV), a single draw can cost 18.5% of NAV — a deliberate tradeoff so even a small pool can offer a prize worth playing for (recovery: ~251 tickets, §4.4).

S4 — Nothing on the balance sheet depends on an oracle price. The vault's reserves are USDC. Cards bought back from winners (§2.3) are the one exception, booked at zero value against a price the pool itself just paid — pure upside once re-listed. Payout math runs on the discounted price and rejects feeds with too much uncertainty, so an oracle mistake is charged to one draw's payout only.

S5 — The solver refuses cleanly. If RTP target, refund floor, and bucket caps can't all be satisfied with current inventory, no table gets published.

S6 — Withdrawal discipline. The 7-day cooldown, plus withdrawals only drawing against C - L, prevents jackpot-timing exits.

S7 — Consignment isolation. Consigned cards are never added into pool value; cash owed against them is fully reserved. Shelf capacity only grows as LP capital does.

S8 — Mispricing is bounded and frozen in place. The oracle price is read once at listing time, the payout fixed by rule, and cards freeze into the table and ticket without ever being re-read at claim — no drift from claim timing or later price moves. A bad price could still skew odds for tables built after it lands; the confidence discount, staleness rule, admission threshold, and 600-second circuit breaker (§6) limit how far. Because the instant buyback is anchored to the frozen listing-time P, not a live re-read, a bad price costs a single draw instead of compounding into repeated LP losses.

S9 — Bulk reservation on card-guaranteed tables (design direction, not genesis). If turned on (§2.1), each table would reserve enough bulk cards to cover every open ticket, and stop selling if inventory couldn't keep up — an inventory obligation, not a cash one.

Solvency and yield are separate claims: the invariants above bound insolvency — stress-tested across a full range of scenarios, see VERIFICATION.md — but don't promise LP returns stay attractive through a downturn.

6. Architecture and the oracle#

The protocol is three Anchor programs (Anchor is the standard Solana smart-contract framework). Splitting them is a security decision: money (vault) and game logic (engine) sit behind a narrow interface, and the price feed is a plug-in either program can read but neither can fake.

None is meant to stay exclusive — cranks are open to anyone, the publisher is a replaceable plug-in, the frontend competes with any other app on the same programs. Treasury-held escrow is the one real trust cost, with an on-chain exit path (§3.2); roles are combined because the protocol is new, not because the design requires it.

6.2 The oracle registry#

Every card resolves to a price feed: price, confidence width, last-update time, governance-approved publisher. Programs read only through the registry — feeds can eventually be combined across publishers instead of trusting one.

Three defenses limit damage from a bad price:

  • Confidence discount. Payouts use the low end of the confidence band, \hat p_a = p_a - \sigma_a. Feeds above a threshold (\kappa = 0.96) are excluded outright.
  • Staleness. A stale price is excluded from new tables; prices are read once at table build, then frozen.
  • Circuit breaker. A price jump past a threshold holds pending until a confirming update arrives 600 seconds later.

Because the bankroll never holds a card whose value depends on the oracle (§5, S4), a bad price only affects one draw's payout — never a balance-sheet liability.

6.3 Governance#

Governance at genesis is a multisig. Every dial in Appendix A is a configurable genesis parameter with a stated bound, movable only inside a published, on-chain range. The planned successor is futarchy via MetaDAO: a governance token, sold once the protocol has real mainnet volume, claims the protocol-fee treasury (§4.5) and steers the bounded dials through prediction markets. The token never touches the yield loop — HOUSE shares keep the vault claim, the token only owns the fee treasury, bounded the same way. The Olympus lesson intact: the governance market prices the fee line, not the house.

Appendix B: deferred mechanisms#

Specified in earlier drafts and companion documents; not part of the base protocol at genesis.

Card-guaranteed tables. The consolation bucket could pay in cards instead of cash, so every draw returns a card. Genesis launches with cash floors only; this ships once bulk card inventory is deep enough. Mechanics: for a bulk floor value b (cheapest listable card, working assumption $20), a miss would deliver n = \lfloor fc/b \rfloor cards plus fc - nb cash — the same floor value, just paid differently. A table would only guarantee a card on tickets priced above roughly b/f (about $155 at $20 bulk cards and a 13% floor); below that, cash. Would pair with S9 (Appendix, §5): each such table would reserve enough bulk cards to cover every open ticket, and stop selling if inventory couldn't keep up.

Bundles. An N-draw ticket at price N \cdot c, one randomness request, guaranteeing at least K of N outcomes are cards. Paid for inside the same RTP budget — the guarantee doesn't come free, it leans the jackpot bucket down and the guaranteed slots toward bulk cards. Unlike the industry's server-side "pity" counters, the guarantee is a published constraint anyone can verify.

Volatility flavors. Two tables per price point, same price and RTP target, different shape via the floor dial: Standard (25–30% floor, tightened jackpot) vs. Degen (13% floor, maximum jackpot). No new solver logic — just different bucket-cap settings — but each flavor is its own live table (~12 cards), so genesis ships one table per price point.

Advance on listing. Because listings sit in escrow, the pool could advance a consignor up to 70% of their payout P at listing time rather than on draw — structurally a secured loan at ~1.5× coverage, repaid from the draw payout or in full to delist. Total advances would be capped as a fraction of liquid USDC so they never compete with cash locked against open tickets. Buys timing, not more shelf capacity.

Direct card deposits into the bankroll — removed from v1 scope entirely. Earlier plans included oracle pricing, haircut-credited share minting, and a regime-scaled cap ladder; consignment ships without any of that. Consign-then-pay-in-shares (§3.5) is the only path into card equity at launch.

Consignor-held custody with a delivery bond — the card never leaves the owner's wallet; delivery is guaranteed economically instead of by escrow. The consignor would post a bond B = \max((V_f - P)(1+m), \phi V_f), margin m decaying from 50% toward 25% with a clean history, floor \phi = 10\%. If the consignor reneges, the winner receives the bond, topped up by the pool to V_f; since B \ge V_f - P by construction, the pool's added cost is capped at P. Superseded at genesis by direct escrow (§3.2).

Haircut-rebate vesting — only meaningful alongside direct card deposits; deferred with them.

Per-tier oracle circuit breakers — a single conservative global breaker ships first.

Oracle-priced standing buyback — a continuous pool bid on any eligible card at a fraction of oracle value. Stays deferred: pricing a standing bid directly off the oracle is exactly the design that turns a price mistake into repeated LP losses. The instant buyback that did ship (§2.3) is different — priced off a real consignment sale, scoped to a just-won card, time-limited.

Sponsored overlays — third parties fund bonus prize buckets at published odds. Marketing budgets as provable player value.

Retention policy — streak bonuses, daily rolls, volume tiers, leaderboard prizes: treasury programs funded from the protocol fee, never the RTP budget or reserves, changeable without a program upgrade.

Referrer / distribution fee — a per-ticket fee for third-party frontends that bring in players; a candidate future addition, not part of genesis.
